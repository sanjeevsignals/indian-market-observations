Martingale: The Beautiful Lie

How a 300-year-old gambling scheme became the most dangerous idea in retail trading — and why its real victims are not unlucky. They are mathematically certain.

Sanjeev Singh Sanjeev Signals March 2026 ~5,400 words
Part I  The Origin of a Seduction

A Casino Floor in 18th-Century France, and the Idea That Never Died

In the gambling halls of 18th-century France, somewhere between the clatter of roulette wheels and the haze of pipe smoke, a betting system was born. Its logic was devastatingly simple: double your bet after every loss, and the first win you land will recover everything you have previously surrendered — plus a small profit equal to your original stake. The system needed no expertise, no edge, no forecasting ability. You merely needed patience and capital. To a gambler who had spent the evening watching his chips drain away, it must have felt like a mathematical salvation.

The word itself carries a curious etymology. The Provençal expression jouga a la martegalo translates, roughly, as "to play in an absurd and incomprehensible way." A French-English dictionary from the 17th century lists the term "à la martingale" with the meaning: absurdly, foolishly, grossly. Long before the mathematics caught up to it, the common tongue already understood something that our modern trading screens seem to forget. The probabilist Jean Ville formalized the mathematical concept in his 1939 doctoral thesis, building upon earlier work by Paul Lévy, who had introduced the idea to probability theory in 1934. Joseph Leo Doob later developed the rigorous mathematical framework, including the Optional Stopping Theorem — a result that formally proved what the Provençal tongue had always suspected: the strategy is, at its foundation, a dead end.

History added legend to mathematics. In 1891, a gambler named Charles De Ville Wells — later immortalized as "The Man Who Broke the Bank at Monte Carlo" — reportedly won over one million francs at the roulette tables. Crowds whispered that he had discovered the ultimate system. What they did not see was the Monte Carlo return trip, when Wells lost everything he had won. The system had not been broken. The man had merely been lucky for a brief interval, and the house collected what was owed at the end.

Yet here we are, in 2026, watching retail traders discover Martingale on YouTube videos and Telegram groups as though it were a freshly unearthed secret. The strategy has migrated from the roulette wheel to the NIFTY 50 options chain, from the casino floor to the Zerodha terminal. The geography has changed. The mathematics has not moved an inch.

The laws of probability are not negotiable. They do not care whether you are betting on red at Monte Carlo or buying a NIFTY CE at a round-number support. The mathematics of ruin applies with equal indifference to both.

Part II  The Mechanics of the Trap

Why the Logic Feels Unbreakable — And Why That Feeling Is Precisely the Danger

To understand why Martingale seduces so many otherwise intelligent people, one must begin by acknowledging that its logic is not stupid. It is, within a narrow and fictional set of assumptions, mathematically coherent. If you had infinite capital, if no broker or exchange imposed margin limits, and if you had unlimited time, the strategy would be provably profitable. The chance that you will eventually flip heads — or catch one winning trade — approaches certainty as the number of attempts approaches infinity. This is a mathematical fact. It is also entirely useless in the real world, because real traders have finite capital, finite margin, and finite time before a losing streak bankrupts the account.

Let us trace the arithmetic with the brutal honesty it deserves. A trader begins with a position of ₹10,000. The trade fails. Logic — or rather, the seductive logic of Martingale — says: double next time. The sequence that follows is not abstract mathematics; it is the biography of thousands of blown trading accounts.

Martingale Escalation — Starting Position ₹10,000 per Trade
Trade 1 ₹10,000 at risk Loss → double
Trade 2 ₹20,000 at risk Loss → double
Trade 3 ₹40,000 at risk Loss → double
Trade 4 ₹80,000 at risk Loss → double
Trade 5 ₹1,60,000 at risk Loss → double
Trade 6 ₹3,20,000 at risk Loss → double
Cumulative ₹6,30,000 committed — to recover a ₹10,000 profit After 6 consecutive losses

Six consecutive losses. It sounds unlikely. Most traders, when they read that sequence, feel a quiet confidence: it will not happen to them. This confidence is itself a cognitive bias — one with a name, a neurological basis, and a documented history of destroying accounts. When statisticians modeled a 200-session run at a European roulette wheel, they found an 84% probability that a losing streak of six or more consecutive outcomes would occur at some point during those 200 sessions. A trader who believes six consecutive bad trades are "impossible" is not reading probability correctly. They are reading hope.

After ten consecutive losses — a scenario that carries approximately an 11% chance of occurring within a 200-session run — the required position has escalated to 1,023 times the original stake. To sustain a Martingale strategy through 5,000 sessions with a failure probability below 10%, a trader would need capital exceeding 65,500 times their initial position. A trader risking ₹10,000 per trade would require over ₹65 crore in reserve capital. This is not a conservative estimate designed to frighten. It is the output of the same probability mathematics that the strategy itself claims to rely on.

The Contractual Nature of Ruin

The Optional Stopping Theorem — the formal mathematical result developed by Doob in the mid-20th century — proves that no stopping strategy can improve the expected value of a Martingale sequence when the underlying game carries a negative expected value. In financial markets, every trade carries friction: brokerage commission, exchange fees, impact cost, the bid-ask spread. These are individually small. They are relentless cumulatively. Even a strategy with genuine edge, if layered onto a Martingale position-sizing scheme, will see that edge consumed by exponential capital requirements long before the sample size is sufficient for expectancy to manifest.

The real danger is not that Martingale fails spectacularly on the first attempt. The real danger is that it works reliably for months — or even years — until the single losing streak that the probability tables always promised arrives. This creates a survivorship bias that makes the strategy appear vindicated. Traders who have used it without catastrophe share their success. Those who blew their accounts are silent. The community learns from the survivors, not the casualties, and the cycle of seduction continues.

Critical Distinction — Markets vs. Roulette

A roulette wheel offers fixed, knowable odds on every spin. Financial markets do not. A market can trend against a position for weeks, months, or years. An option can expire to zero, terminating the entire sequence permanently regardless of position size. Tesla declined for four consecutive months in 2022, falling from approximately $298 to $119. A Martingale trader averaging down through that move would have required capital that most retail accounts cannot conceive of holding. This is the additional dimension of risk that distinguishes trading from casino games: the game itself can end with a permanent, irreversible loss of capital.

Part III  The Neuroscience of Loss Chasing

The Brain That Cannot Stop Doubling Down

If Martingale is so clearly flawed, why does it retain such a grip on the imagination of retail traders? The answer is not simply ignorance. It is neurology. The human brain is not equipped to reason intuitively about long-run probability sequences. It is equipped to find patterns, to seek justice, to believe that sustained bad luck is a debt that reality owes us. These instincts were adaptive on the African savannah. In a derivatives market, they are ruinous.

Research using functional magnetic resonance imaging has revealed what happens in the brain immediately after a financial loss. When a person loses a bet and must decide their next action, the frontoparietal network — responsible for executive, goal-directed thinking — shows markedly increased activation. Simultaneously, the caudate nucleus and the ventral striatum, which support the normal learning mechanism associating outcomes with behavior, show decreased activity. In plain terms: after a loss, the part of the brain governing rational risk evaluation is suppressed, and the part driving aggressive, goal-oriented behavior is amplified. The neurological state that follows a loss is precisely the state most likely to produce Martingale-style decision-making.

The striatum ordinarily functions as a behavioral correction mechanism. After a loss, it should register the outcome and reduce the probability of repeating the behavior. In individuals exhibiting what researchers term "Gambler's Fallacy-like behavior," this correction mechanism is impaired. The brain does not retreat from the losing strategy. It commits more deeply to it, driven by the conviction — entirely untethered from probability — that a loss must soon be balanced by a win.

This conviction has a name in cognitive psychology: the Representativeness Heuristic, identified by Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman. The brain evaluates the likelihood of a future event by comparing it to known patterns. It expects small samples to reflect the properties of large ones. A streak of five losses feels "wrong" because the brain knows that in a large sample, wins and losses should be roughly balanced. The heuristic then concludes that a win is "due." This is the core fallacy. Markets, like coins, carry no memory of what they did yesterday. Each trade is an independent event. The market does not owe any trader a winning session.

Trading is not risk management. It is nervous system management. The Martingale is dangerous not only because it fails mathematically — though it does — but because it exploits exactly the neurological state that a losing streak creates.

Dopamine, Loss, and the Architecture of Addiction

There is a further complication. Research has established that, for individuals with problem gambling tendencies, losing money triggers dopamine release at levels comparable to winning. The neurological reward is not attached exclusively to the positive outcome — it is attached to the act of continued engagement itself. This is the mechanism that transforms an observer of markets into a captive of them. The trader who continues to double down is not merely making a mathematical error. They are, at a neurological level, being rewarded by the act of continuing. The brain is not processing "I am losing money." It is processing "I am still in the game." The dopaminergic system does not distinguish between these two states with any reliability.

The implications for Martingale specifically are severe. The strategy provides exactly the structure that maximally exploits this reward architecture. After each loss, the trader faces a binary choice: stop and accept defeat, or double and remain engaged. The neurological cost of stopping — the dopamine withdrawal from removing oneself from the game — is immediate and visceral. The mathematical probability of ruin that follows from doubling is felt, if at all, only in the distant future. The brain performs the calculation in favor of continuation, every single time, until the account is empty.

Researchers at the University of Cambridge found that certain structural elements of gambling design — near-misses, the illusion of personal control, unpredictable reward intervals — amplify this effect by creating the impression that skill is involved where chance alone operates. A Martingale trader experiences exactly this illusion. Doubling the position feels like a strategic response. It feels like discipline. It feels like conviction. It is, in structural terms, identical to inserting a second note into a machine that has just taken the first, and telling yourself that this time you have a plan.

Part IV  The Professional Standard

How Serious Capital Is Actually Managed

The contrast between Martingale logic and professional risk management is not a matter of preference or style. It is a structural, institutional difference — one that every major proprietary trading firm, funded account program, and serious hedge fund has arrived at independently, by separate routes, and confirmed through hard experience. Martingale-style position escalation is incompatible with the long-term survival of a trading account. It is not banned because institutions are overly conservative. It is banned because its mathematical properties make capital preservation impossible over any meaningful time horizon.

Prop firms detect Martingale behavior through automated monitoring systems that flag patterns of increasing lot sizes following consecutive losses. The consequence in virtually every funded account program is immediate account termination. FTMO, The5ers, MFF, BrightFunded, Funding Pips — the list of firms with explicit Martingale prohibitions is essentially coextensive with the list of reputable funded programs. This is not coincidental. It reflects a hard-earned industry understanding of what risk profiles are compatible with institutional capital and which ones are not.

The Professional Alternative: Fixed Fractional Risk

Professional traders do not approach position sizing as a mechanism for recovering losses. They approach it as a mechanism for ensuring survival. The foundational principle is simple and invariant: risk a fixed percentage of current account equity on each trade, and let the expectancy of the strategy compound over time. The specific percentage varies by trader, market, and strategy, but the industry standard sits between one and two percent of total equity per trade. This is not timidity — it is a mathematical recognition that survival is the prerequisite for all profit.

The mathematics behind this approach were formalized by John Kelly Jr. at Bell Labs in 1956. The Kelly Criterion provides a formula for calculating the optimal fraction of capital to risk on each position, given a known win rate and reward-to-risk ratio. The formula is: f* = (bp − q) / b, where b is the net gain-to-loss ratio, p is the probability of a win, and q is the probability of a loss. Most professional traders apply a fractional Kelly — typically 25% to 50% of the full Kelly value — to account for model uncertainty and to reduce volatility in the equity curve. The full Kelly, while mathematically optimal for long-run geometric growth, produces drawdowns that most practitioners cannot sustain psychologically.

The critical distinction between the Kelly framework and Martingale is not merely quantitative. It is philosophical. Kelly-based position sizing increases allocation when the edge is strong and reduces it when conditions deteriorate. It is an anti-Martingale system: it scales with demonstrated success, not with demonstrated failure. This mirrors the actual structure of market advantage. When a strategy is working, conditions presumably favor its execution. When a strategy is failing, the rational response is to reduce exposure, not amplify it. Martingale inverts this entirely: it demands maximum capital commitment at the precise moment when the strategy has most clearly demonstrated its failure.

Fixed Percentage Risk per Trade

Typically 1–2% of current equity, recalculated before each trade. This ensures that a losing streak reduces position size proportionally, preventing any single run of bad trades from terminating the account. Ten consecutive losses at 2% risk produces a drawdown of approximately 18%. Not account termination.

Stop Loss Placed at Market Structure

The stop loss is placed where market structure invalidates the trade thesis — a key swing low, a significant support zone, a prior area of absorption. Position size is then calculated backward from the stop distance. Risk drives position size. Position size never drives risk.

Volatility-Adjusted Sizing via ATR

Advanced frameworks adjust position size based on the Average True Range of the instrument. Higher volatility produces smaller positions; lower volatility allows larger ones. Dollar risk per trade remains consistent even as market conditions shift.

Anti-Martingale Scaling During Drawdown

Professional systems reduce position size during drawdown periods, not increase it. This is the direct inversion of the Martingale principle. When a strategy is underperforming, the appropriate response is to reduce exposure until conditions normalize or the edge can be re-validated with fresh analysis.

Expectancy Compounding Over a Meaningful Sample

The goal of professional position management is to survive long enough for statistical edge to manifest. A positive-expectancy strategy risking 1% per trade will, over 200 trades, produce its expected result with high statistical confidence. A Martingale strategy over the same 200 trades carries an 84% probability of encountering a destroying losing streak.

Part V  The Deeper Problem

Martingale Is Not a Strategy. It Is a Belief System.

The persistence of Martingale in retail trading culture is not explained by ignorance alone. Plenty of traders who understand its mathematical properties continue to use it or to defend it. The reason is that Martingale is not, at its root, a position-sizing methodology. It is a psychological position. It is the operationalized belief that the market owes you a recovery. It is loss aversion — one of the most documented and robust findings in behavioral economics — expressed directly in trade entry logic.

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's Prospect Theory established that, in human decision-making, losses loom approximately twice as large as equivalent gains. The pain of losing ₹10,000 is neurologically comparable to the pleasure of gaining ₹20,000. This asymmetry has profound consequences for trading behavior. It means that the human brain is systemically inclined to hold losses — or to compound them — in order to avoid the psychological reality of crystallizing a defeat. Martingale is the most elaborate expression of this tendency. Instead of simply holding a losing position, the trader commits additional capital in the form of a new, larger trade. The loss is not merely held; it is actively pursued.

This is why experienced traders describe Martingale as "loss chasing disguised as strategy." The disguise is effective because it borrows the language of discipline and methodology. The trader is not panicking; they are executing a system. They are not hoping; they are following a protocol. The emotional reality — the refusal to accept that the trade was wrong, the compulsive need to recoup — is hidden behind a mathematical veneer. The veneer is real, in the sense that the arithmetic is genuine. But the psychological driver beneath it is identical to that of a gambler inserting another note into a machine that has already taken the previous bet.

The Problem of Undefined Edge

One of the most important objections to Martingale in a trading context is rarely articulated with sufficient precision. In a casino game, the probability of each outcome is fixed and known. A roulette wheel offers a 47.4% chance of winning a red/black bet in European roulette. This is the baseline against which Martingale mathematics is developed. In financial markets, no such fixed probability exists. The probability of a trade succeeding is not a constant; it varies with market condition, instrument, time of day, macroeconomic regime, liquidity profile, and dozens of additional factors. Many traders who apply Martingale are not doing so to a 50/50 game with a small negative edge. They are applying it to a market that, during a trending regime, may produce directional runs of 10, 15, or 20 consecutive adverse sessions against their bias.

An options buyer faces additional complications that a roulette player does not. Options have expiry. If a NIFTY CE averaged down through a Martingale scheme expires in-the-money, the trader recovers. If it expires worthless, the entire compounded position — not merely the original stake — is lost permanently and with finality. There is no additional doubling possible. The roulette wheel does not expire. The Martingale assumption of "eventually winning" is structurally impossible in a product that can go to zero with mathematical certainty.

The Specific Danger for Options Buyers

Options carry three simultaneous dimensions of risk: direction, time, and volatility. A trader averaging down on a losing options position through Martingale logic is not merely betting on directional recovery. They are also betting against the continued decay of time value and, in many cases, against an adverse shift in implied volatility. Each successive position in the Martingale sequence buys an option that is structurally less valuable than the previous one — it has less time remaining, possibly less favorable strikes, and potentially inflated implied volatility from the adverse move. The mathematical compounding of Martingale capital requirements meets the mathematical certainty of theta decay. The result is not merely probable loss. It is structured, accelerated destruction of capital.

Part VI  The Honest Comparison

Martingale vs. Professional Risk Management: A Direct Analysis

No theoretical argument lands as clearly as a direct comparison. The table below contrasts the core properties of Martingale position management against the professional standard across every dimension that determines long-term trading survival.

Dimension Martingale Logic Professional Standard
Position size determination Driven by outcome of previous trade Driven by account equity and stop distance
Response to a losing streak Increase exposure exponentially Reduce exposure; reassess edge validity
Response to a winning streak Return to minimum; reset cycle Scale gradually with proven equity growth
Maximum single-trade risk Unbounded — grows geometrically Fixed percentage; typically 1–2% of equity
Required capital assumption Theoretically infinite for guaranteed safety Current account equity; no artificial floor
Risk of ruin after 10 losses Account-ending at any realistic capital level ~18% drawdown at 2% risk per trade
Prop firm compliance Universally prohibited; instant disqualification Foundation of every funded account program
Psychological driver Refusal to accept loss; emotional commitment Indifference to individual trade outcomes
Compatibility with options Catastrophically incompatible — expiry ends the sequence permanently Compatible with instruments carrying defined maximum loss
Long-run mathematical expectancy Identical to the underlying strategy; zero improvement Maximizes geometric growth of a positive-expectancy edge

The table above is not merely an argument against a bad strategy. It is a map of two entirely different relationships with the market. Martingale is a reactive posture: the market acts, the trader responds by escalating commitment. Professional position management is a systematic posture: the plan is established before the trade, and the market's behavior determines outcome but never determines the next position size. The first approach places the market in control of capital allocation. The second places the trader in control. These are not different risk tolerances. They are different philosophies about what trading is.

Part VII  The Institutional Verdict

What Prop Firms Are Actually Measuring When They Ban Martingale

When a prop firm bans Martingale strategies, it is not making a moral judgment about greed or gambling. It is making a risk management decision that emerges from the same mathematics governing their own capital allocation. A prop firm's business model requires that the traders it funds produce consistent, positive returns over time. A Martingale trader produces a smooth equity curve punctuated, periodically and inevitably, by total capital destruction. From a risk management perspective, this is not a trading strategy. It is a deferred liability.

Automated monitoring systems used by firms such as The5ers, MFF, and Funding Pips detect specific behavioral signatures: lot sizes that increase following consecutive losses, trades on the same instrument that stack without directional logic, position escalation patterns consistent with Martingale or grid sequences. The consequence of detection is not a warning. It is immediate account termination. This is not punitive. It is structural. A strategy that requires exponential capital escalation is incompatible with a finite funded account by definition. The firm is simply enforcing the mathematics that the trader has declined to confront.

The deeper point is this: the institutional ban on Martingale is not an opinion. It is the collective, hard-won wisdom of organizations that manage capital at scale and have learned, through direct experience, what trading behaviors are compatible with long-run survival and which are not. No serious money manager, institutional trader, or proprietary firm applies Martingale-style position escalation to client or firm capital. This is not coincidence. It is consensus derived from mathematics.

Part VIII  The Honest Path Forward

Survival, Edge, and the Compounding of Discipline

The goal of professional trading is not to recover losses quickly. This is the mindset of the gambler, and it produces the gambler's outcomes. The goal is to survive long enough, with capital sufficiently intact, that the statistical edge embedded in a valid trading approach has the opportunity to manifest across a meaningful sample of trades. This is a different objective entirely, and it requires a different psychology entirely.

A strategy with a 55% win rate and a 1.5:1 reward-to-risk ratio carries a positive expectancy. Over any single trade, it will lose 45% of the time. Over 100 trades, it will produce reliable, compounding growth — provided the position sizing is designed to survive the inevitable losing sequences that probability guarantees. The difference between a trader who captures that growth and one who does not is rarely edge quality. It is capital management during drawdown.

Drawdowns are not anomalies. They are mathematical requirements. Even the best institutional strategies produce periods of negative performance. The defining characteristic of professional capital management is that drawdown periods do not end the game. Martingale, by contrast, guarantees that a sufficiently severe drawdown will be account-ending regardless of the underlying strategy's quality. It converts a temporary setback into a permanent catastrophe by demanding escalating capital commitment at precisely the moment when that capital is most at risk.

The Practice of Structured Indifference

The psychological shift that distinguishes a professional trader from a Martingale practitioner is what might be called structured indifference to individual trade outcomes. The professional genuinely does not require any single trade to succeed. The outcome of today's trade is not a referendum on the validity of the system, the intelligence of the trader, or the fairness of the market. It is one data point in a distribution that, over time, will reflect the underlying expectancy of the approach. This mental posture — cultivated deliberately, held systematically — is what allows a trader to take a loss on Monday, execute the same setup on Tuesday with unchanged commitment, and maintain that discipline across the months it takes for statistical edge to produce consistent results.

Martingale makes this posture impossible by design. It attaches the size of the next position to the outcome of the previous one. It makes each trade's result matter enormously — not just psychologically, but mathematically. The strategy demands that the trader care about every loss, respond to every loss, escalate at every loss. It institutionalizes exactly the emotional reactivity that professional trading requires a trader to extinguish. It is, in this sense, not merely a bad strategy. It is an anti-discipline system that actively reinforces the psychological habits most corrosive to long-term performance.

The market does not owe you a recovery. The sooner a trader internalizes this, the sooner they stop designing strategies around the assumption that it does.

Part IX  The Final Accounting

What Martingale Really Costs — and What It Is Really Selling

Let us be precise about the transaction that Martingale offers a trader. On the debit side of the ledger: a mathematically guaranteed probability of account destruction within any realistic trading timeframe, a capital requirement that no retail trader can realistically meet, and a psychology that systematically reinforces the worst trading behaviors. On the credit side: a series of small wins, a temporarily smooth equity curve, and the emotional comfort of feeling that each loss is solvable by the next trade.

This is not a strategy. It is an installment plan for account loss, paid in comfortable monthly increments until the single catastrophic payment arrives and terminates the account. The research is unequivocal: all betting systems, including Martingale, lead ultimately to the same mathematical expectation of gain per unit wagered as the underlying game. The position-sizing scheme cannot improve the underlying expectancy. If the edge is positive, it does not need Martingale. If the edge is negative, Martingale cannot rescue it — it merely delays and then amplifies the reckoning.

The traders who read this article and continue using Martingale are not, in most cases, unintelligent. They have been sold a feeling, and that feeling is real even if its mathematical basis is not. The feeling of control. The feeling that a loss is never truly final. The feeling that the system has an answer for adversity. These are powerful rewards, and the brain distributes them freely. The market, when the inevitable losing streak arrives, takes them back with compounded interest.

The traders who read this article and abandon Martingale are not necessarily smarter. They have developed the capacity to act on mathematical reality rather than psychological comfort. They have accepted that losses are permanent, that winning streaks do not owe them anything, and that the only sustainable response to a loss is a proportional position on the next trade — not a larger one. They have chosen survival over the appearance of recovery. Over time, survival compounds into something that feels far better than the brief comfort of doubling down ever could.

In the end, the Martingale system offers traders the one thing that no legitimate market strategy can provide: the promise that discipline is unnecessary, that losses are always temporary, and that a clever position-sizing rule can substitute for genuine edge. That promise is three hundred years old. It has been tested on roulette wheels in Monte Carlo, on currency desks in London, and on NIFTY 50 options screens in Mumbai. It has produced the same result, at every scale and in every era, with the mathematical consistency of a physical law.

The market has no memory. Every trade is new. The account that ends in ruin was not unlucky. It was precisely as lucky as the mathematics always promised it would be.

Three Principles That Replace Martingale Thinking

Rather than close with only a critique, it is worth stating clearly what the professional alternative looks like in operational terms. These are not abstract guidelines. They are executable practices that form the foundation of every serious trading framework.

The first principle is that position size must be determined by account equity and market structure — never by the outcome of a previous trade. The size of the next trade has zero logical relationship to whether the last trade won or lost. Linking them is the defining error of Martingale, and refusing that linkage is the defining discipline of professional trading.

The second principle is that the stop loss must be placed at the point where market structure tells you the trade thesis is wrong. Not where you can afford the loss. Not where it will not hurt too much. At the precise level where the market is telling you that your premise was incorrect. This distinction — between a stop placed by market structure and a stop placed by emotional affordability — is the difference between a trade and a hope.

The third principle is that the purpose of trading capital is to remain in the game across a statistically meaningful sample of trades. A single trade, however large, does not define performance. A year of consistent, disciplined execution — with each loss limited to one or two percent of equity, with each win allowed to run to its structural conclusion — produces the compounding that makes trading a viable long-term endeavor. The goal is not to recover losses quickly. The goal is to never need to.
